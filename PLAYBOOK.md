# Playbook: Founder-Led Content → Pipeline

**A 90-day SOP for early-stage B2B SaaS (pre-$1M ARR, founder is still the best salesperson).**

Built from the 46 artifacts collected in [`research/`](research/) — 10 YouTube transcripts and 36 LinkedIn posts from 10 benchmark creators. Every recommendation below cites the specific artifact it came from, linked to both the live source and the archived copy in this repo. Anything not traceable to a source is labelled **[ORIGINAL]**.

---

## Why this topic, and what I deliberately cut

The research set spans positioning, demand gen, sales craft, AI ops and audience building. That is too wide to be actionable. I narrowed to the one problem where my sources overlap most densely and where sequencing errors are most expensive: **a technical founder with a working product, no case studies, no audience, and no marketing hire, who needs the content they publish to actually produce sales conversations.**

**In scope:** positioning, message testing, LinkedIn/YouTube publishing, converting attention into conversations, what to automate and when.
**Out of scope:** paid acquisition, enterprise ABM, PLG self-serve funnels, consumer. Where my sources speak to those, I say so rather than stretching them.

**The core thesis — and it is mine, not any single source's:** every creator in this set sells one slice of the pipeline and therefore presents their slice as the starting point. Assembled honestly, their advice implies a strict order, and most founders run it backwards. **Positioning before publishing. Warm network before cold. Manual before automated.** The stage gates below are the deliverable; the tactics inside them are borrowed and cited.

## Stage 0 — Do not publish yet (Week 1–2)

**0.1 — Write down your competitive alternatives before anything else.** Not your problem statement, not your vision. What the customer would actually do if you did not exist, including "spreadsheet" and "nothing." Dunford treats this as the input that makes every later step possible, and names sales reps — not founders or product — as the most reliable source for what shows up on real shortlists *(source: April Dunford, [YouTube 02.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9pf_iEomjY) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/april-dunford/D9pf_iEomjY.md))*.

**0.2 — Convert capabilities into differentiated value by asking "so what?" repeatedly.** Dunford calls differentiated value the most important and most time-consuming step of a positioning exercise, and warns that "make money / save money" alone does not differentiate *(same source)*.

**0.3 — Take a public position on where the market is going, separate from your product vision.** Dunford's argument: in a fast-changing market buyers do their own homework but still want to know what *you* think, and she contrasts Altman's "sold like electricity," Amodei's "AI will write 90% of code," and Nadella's "models are becoming a commodity… the intelligence layer is only as good as the context you give it" as three different, deliberate market POVs *(source: April Dunford, [LinkedIn 13.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7470826617833967616/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/april-dunford/7470826617833967616.md))*. This POV becomes your content spine in Stage 2.

**0.4 — Test the positioning in live sales calls, not on your homepage.** Dunford is unambiguous that landing-page A/B testing is "a terrible way to test it": you end up testing page design and traffic mix rather than positioning, and in B2B "you just don't actually have that much traffic to actually get to a test that's sort of significant." Her replacement: translate positioning into a structured sales pitch, have your *best* rep (at this stage, you) run it against pre-qualified best-fit prospects, and iterate until the rep prefers it to the old pitch *(source: April Dunford, [YouTube 02.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9pf_iEomjY) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/april-dunford/D9pf_iEomjY.md))*.

**0.5 — Sit in those calls and write down every question asked.** Dunford describes doing exactly this in-house — recording every question, then de-briefing after each pitch to cut words that misfire and rebuild diagrams that confuse *(same source)*. **This transcript of questions is the single most valuable asset you will produce in 90 days.** It is your pitch fix list *and* your content backlog.

**0.6 — Write a messaging document, not a homepage.** Dunford's warning is specifically about drift: teams build the homepage, then a trade-show asset off the homepage, then a campaign off the trade-show asset, and "the next thing you know it's a year later and you've got messaging out there that doesn't look anything like the homepage anymore." A central boilerplate doc keeps everything within one degree *(same source)*.

> **Gate to Stage 1:** you can state your differentiated value in one sentence, you have run the pitch live at least 5 times, and you have ≥20 recorded prospect questions.

## Stage 1 — Your first customers come from warm, not from content (Week 2–6)

Content does not produce your first ten customers. It compounds too slowly and you have no proof to point at yet.

**1.1 — Build the warm list before you build an audience.** Erhart's "bullseye method": 20 minutes writing every name connected to every job, school, sport, club and family business — 30–100 names, each paired with the business they are attached to, no filtering *(source: Adam Erhart, [YouTube 19.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sFSeMVQnsRc) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/adam-erhart/sFSeMVQnsRc.md))*.

**1.2 — Filter by visible, diagnosable pain — not by who you think will buy.** Erhart's point is that identical messages land differently depending on whether the recipient can *feel* the gap; his filter is to audit each business and rank by size of gap, then contact only the top five *(same source)*.

**1.3 — Lead with a finding, not a request.** The reframe is Erhart's: "You're not asking Dave for a favor. You're giving Dave a gift." His message shape — casual opener, "messing around with a tool I use for work," a specific consequence, and a yes/no micro-ask ("want me to send the report?") — is deliberately built to avoid triggering a sales-pitch response *(same source)*.

**1.4 — Never write outreach that implies the recipient is incompetent.** Braun's mechanism: "You're overpaying" / "your broker isn't doing their job" makes people defend themselves, and "the moment someone feels judged, they stop being curious." He replaces blame with a reason a gap can exist even when good people did good work *(source: Josh Braun, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474201299379564544/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/josh-braun/7474201299379564544.md))*. Same rule for the word "why," which he says "puts people on the witness stand" *(source: Josh Braun, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474076171358457858/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/josh-braun/7474076171358457858.md))*.

**1.5 — Write subject lines as problems, not promises.** Braun: "Increase Revenue" / "Unlock Growth" fail because there is no problem in them; "stalled deals," "missing receipts," "CRM adoption" read like an internal note from a colleague *(source: Josh Braun, [LinkedIn 19.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7473743280002973697/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/josh-braun/7473743280002973697.md))*.

**1.6 — Qualify on priority rank, not problem existence.** Braun's dead-pixel-TV argument: he has the problem, it is real, and he will never act on it, because it competes with a broken gate and a cracked window. The question is not "do you have this problem" but where it ranks against everything else competing for budget — and "you can't talk a #17 priority into becoming a #1 priority" *(source: Josh Braun, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474185940131917824/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/josh-braun/7474185940131917824.md))*. **This is the highest-leverage single idea in the entire research set.** It kills more wasted pipeline than any messaging tweak.

**1.7 — Build trust by naming what they should *not* buy.** Braun's mechanic told him he did not need the OEM part and that his tires had 4–5k miles left; the trust came from the seller volunteering the cheaper path. "That's what trustworthy people do. They say, 'Here's what you don't have to buy'" *(source: Josh Braun, [YouTube 17.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9_BeKzJlOXs) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/josh-braun/9_BeKzJlOXs.md))*.

> **Gate to Stage 2:** you have had ≥15 real sales conversations and closed ≥3 customers. You now have proof, objections, and language that survived contact.

## Stage 2 — Publish the objection ledger (Week 4–12, ongoing)

Now content works, because you finally have something only you know.

**2.1 — Source topics from questions people already ask, in three places.** Robinson's method: the best posts from the top 2–3 creators in your niche over the last 90 days, an LLM query for the most common questions in your niche, and Reddit threads *(source: Adam Robinson, [YouTube 16.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eUzqVJpZPP4) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/adam-robinson/eUzqVJpZPP4.md))*. **Add your Stage 0.5 question log as the first and best source** — it is the only one your competitors cannot copy.

**2.1b — Borrow proven structure, then add one new thing.** Robinson's version is a swipe file: spend two hours pulling hooks, formats and closes from the best posts in your space, then "take a structure or architecture of a post that already works and put your own story or angle on top of it" *(source: Adam Robinson, [YouTube 16.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eUzqVJpZPP4) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/adam-robinson/eUzqVJpZPP4.md))*. Mark Pincus, via Lenny, gives the same idea a sharper frame — "Proven, Better, New": become expert in what already works and copy it, improve it enough that the audience would switch, then add something untried. He calls copying well "moral arbitrage," on the grounds that resistance to copying is a school-taught instinct rather than a useful one *(source: Lenny Rachitsky / Mark Pincus, [YouTube 14.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7eh9C3TUotc) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/lenny-rachitsky/7eh9C3TUotc.md); also [LinkedIn 15.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7472321367086899200/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/lenny-rachitsky/7472321367086899200.md))*. Two independent sources landing on the same mechanic is the strongest convergence in the set.

**2.1c — Hold a publishing bar and write it down.** Lenny's public criteria for guest posts double as a usable quality gate: is it highly actionable and concrete, is it well-researched or rooted in something you uniquely know, does it add something new to the conversation — with two stated disqualifiers, promoting your own product and being "high on pontification/theory, low on tactics/specifics" *(source: Lenny Rachitsky, [LinkedIn 16.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7472710202161524736/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/lenny-rachitsky/7472710202161524736.md))*. Run every draft against those five tests before it ships.

**2.2 — Aim for the "prolific zone."** Robinson's frame: mainstream takes are noise, and takes that are too far out get you written off, so target the band where people who agree love it and people who disagree push back. His own example — arguing the predictable-revenue/BDR model "was born in 2012 and died in 2023" — is what made the post spread *(same source)*.

**2.3 — Write the hook as if the reader has never heard of you.** Robinson: the hook's only job is to earn the "see more" click, nothing else — not to summarise, not to set up the list. He builds credibility with specific numbers ("I bootstrapped 20 million ARR in 3 and 1/2 years") because most readers arrive with zero context on who you are *(same source)*.

**2.4 — Structure the body: agitate a specific pain → reframe or solve → give one takeaway.** Robinson's three-beat structure, described as "a mini hero's journey in the space of a few bullet points" *(same source)*.

**2.5 — Close with something that makes them feel, not a manufactured engagement bait.** Robinson explicitly calls out "let's continue the conversation in the comments" as momentum-killing *(same source)*.

**2.6 — Teach one move per post.** The observable pattern across Braun's 10 collected posts is single-idea discipline: reframing, features-vs-benefits, one subject-line rule, one qualifying question. None of them tries to teach two things *(source: [Josh Braun's 10 posts, 19–20.06.2026](research/linkedin-posts/josh-braun/index.md))*.

**2.7 — Repeat the spine until you are sick of it.** Dunford: establishing a position "requires you to essentially say the same thing over and over and over again until you're really quite sick of it. And at the point where you're quite sick of it is exactly where it's starting to land" *(source: April Dunford, [YouTube 02.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9pf_iEomjY) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/april-dunford/D9pf_iEomjY.md))*.

**2.8 — Atomize long-form into many small posts, and say so.** Lenny's collected week is a clean demonstration: one Mark Pincus interview produced a full episode post, a 10-point takeaway post, and at least two standalone pull-quote posts, each of which stands alone *(source: Lenny Rachitsky, [LinkedIn 14–16.06.2026](research/linkedin-posts/lenny-rachitsky/index.md))*.

**2.9 — Protect the argument and the hook from AI.** Shleyner's distinction, via Gerhardt: AI replaced the *research* half of copywriting — "copy researching," finding anecdotal data and understanding a market — but the writing it produces is "not inspired" *(source: Dave Gerhardt & Eddie Shleyner, [YouTube 18.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VfmYv_QRUpM) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/dave-gerhardt/VfmYv_QRUpM.md))*. Practical rule: AI may do research, outlining, derivatives and repurposing; it may not write your hook, your argument, or any first-person story.

**2.10 — Publish where buying-intent answers get formed, not only where humans scroll.** Patel's claim is that AI systems now assemble a shortlist before a human scrolls, and that classic rank alone is insufficient *(source: Neil Patel, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474159287041249280/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/7474159287041249280.md) and [19.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7473494810994040832/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/7473494810994040832.md))*. **Treat the direction as plausible and the specific figures as unverified** — see [Weaknesses](#weaknesses-of-this-playbook) and the note on Patel in the final section.

## Stage 3 — Measure honestly (from Week 8)

**3.1 — Separate coverage problems from conversion problems.** Lemkin's most useful admission is that his 140%-of-last-year result had at least four candidate causes — concentrating leads into the best closers, going from <40% to 100% inbound coverage, working the dormant CRM, and a market tailwind he calls "a tailwind, not a strategy" — and that "we can't run the A/B test." His conclusion: deploy agents because you have a coverage problem, not because AI closes deals *(source: Jason Lemkin, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474193110495821824/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/jason-lemkin/7474193110495821824.md))*. **Adopt this epistemic standard for your own reporting.**

**3.2 — Run a positioning review on a fixed cadence, not on vibes.** Dunford's in-house practice: a standing check-in roughly every six months with the founder, head of sales and head of product, asking whether competitive alternatives or differentiated capabilities have shifted. If nothing changed, change nothing — she warns explicitly against marketers rewriting messaging out of boredom *(source: April Dunford, [YouTube 02.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9pf_iEomjY) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/april-dunford/D9pf_iEomjY.md))*.

**3.3 — Do not treat engagement as pipeline.** Nothing in the collected set links a post's reactions to revenue. In my own sample the highest-engagement post from a B2B marketing expert was Gerhardt's personal post about his son ("Dad watch this," 136 reactions), which sells nothing *(source: Dave Gerhardt, [LinkedIn 19.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7473691085546905600/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/dave-gerhardt/7473691085546905600.md))*. Reactions measure resonance, not intent.

## Stage 4 — Automate last, and only the capture layer (Week 12+)

**4.1 — Start capturing call transcripts, docs and decisions from day one.** This is the one part of Siu's "company brain" that a small team should adopt immediately: capture is the bottom layer, and everything else depends on it. His claim that 15 calls yielded 390 insights, 470 facts and 125 frameworks illustrates the multiplier from one input *(source: Eric Siu, [YouTube 19.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTuA_uMXfCQ) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/eric-siu/qTuA_uMXfCQ.md))*.

**4.2 — Add a source-of-truth rule before you add agents.** Siu's own caution: AI will trust stale docs and outdated SOPs, freshness and authority hierarchy decide what wins, and — notably — "even recently we have gone wrong" on exactly this *(same source)*.

**4.3 — Close the loop only where you have volume.** Siu's paid-creative loop (agents generate variants, performance data feeds back, winners scale, losers die, no human re-briefs) is a real pattern, but it presumes enough spend to generate signal *(source: Eric Siu, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474174231849684993/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/eric-siu/7474174231849684993.md))*. Below meaningful ad spend, the loop optimises noise.

**4.4 — Expect the "AI budget" filter in your buyer's world.** Lemkin's blunt read: "There is no additional budget for pre-AI workflow tools… stop pretending we're going back to 2024" *(source: Jason Lemkin, [YouTube 11.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pu4IERjQWaM) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/jason-lemkin/Pu4IERjQWaM.md))*. Directionally useful for how you frame value; it is one operator's market call, not a measured fact.

## Where experts disagree

### 1. Cold email: foundational skill, or a trap for beginners?

- **Josh Braun** treats cold outreach as a craft worth mastering, and most of his collected output is mechanics for doing it well — problem-shaped subject lines, non-judgmental framing, priority-rank qualification *(source: [Josh Braun, LinkedIn 19–20.06.2026](research/linkedin-posts/josh-braun/index.md))*.
- **Adam Erhart** opens by rejecting it for beginners outright: "You don't need to send a single cold email to get your first client," because cold email "works because of volume" and a new founder has no case studies, no proof and no patience for 5,000 sends. His sharpest line: "The people teaching cold email now did not start with cold email" *(source: Adam Erhart, [YouTube 19.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sFSeMVQnsRc) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/adam-erhart/sFSeMVQnsRc.md))*.

**My call: Erhart on sequencing, Braun on mechanics — and they are answering different questions.** Erhart is right that cold is a volume game and volume games punish people with no proof; that is why Stage 1 of this playbook is warm-only. But Erhart's own solution quietly depends on Braun's craft — his "gift not favor" message is exactly Braun's don't-make-them-defensive principle applied to a warm list. I reject Erhart's implication that cold email is avoidable forever: his warm list caps out at ~100 names, which is not a growth channel. Use warm to get proof; adopt Braun's mechanics once you have it. **One caution on Erhart:** his advice is delivered inside a funnel for a HighLevel affiliate trial, and the audit step conveniently requires that tool. The method survives without it; do the audits manually.

### 2. What counts as evidence that your message works?

- **April Dunford** rejects landing-page A/B tests for positioning and insists on live sales conversations, because in B2B the traffic is too thin for significance and the test confounds positioning with page design *(source: [YouTube 02.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9pf_iEomjY) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/april-dunford/D9pf_iEomjY.md))*.
- **Neil Patel** runs the opposite epistemology: conversion rate by landing-page type, 60-campaign comparisons of AI vs traditional traffic, 2024-vs-2026 metric shifts *(source: Neil Patel, [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7473857202148032512/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/7473857202148032512.md) and [18.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7473132458570420224/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/7473132458570420224.md))*.

**My call: Dunford, decisively, for the reader of this playbook.** The disagreement is really about sample size, and it resolves on volume. Patel's methods require traffic a pre-$1M company does not have; running an A/B test on 300 sessions produces a number that feels like evidence and is not. Dunford's method yields *why* — you hear the confusion in real time — which is what you actually need before product-market fit. Patel's approach becomes correct later, and I would switch at roughly 5,000 qualified monthly sessions. There is also an asymmetry worth naming: Dunford's claim is a reasoned argument you can audit; Patel's stats ("we looked at 60 campaigns") are unfalsifiable without published methodology.

### 3. AI in content: leverage engine or hollowing agent?

- **Eric Siu** is maximalist — a company brain on 500k tokens of persistent memory, 90+ daily crons, agents publishing posts, and a self-improving creative loop *(source: [YouTube 19.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTuA_uMXfCQ) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/eric-siu/qTuA_uMXfCQ.md))*.
- **Dave Gerhardt & Eddie Shleyner** argue the opposite about the writing itself: AI took over the research, but its prose is "not inspired," and the whole premise of the episode is that copywriting remains a human profession *(source: [YouTube 18.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VfmYv_QRUpM) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/dave-gerhardt/VfmYv_QRUpM.md))*.

**My call: split by layer, and the split is not a compromise — it is where the evidence actually points.** Siu is describing *operations* (retrieval, routing, variant generation, spend optimisation) and Gerhardt is describing *authorship* (the argument, the hook, the story). Both can be right because they are talking about different work. I side with Siu below the waterline and Gerhardt above it. Tellingly, Siu's strongest self-evidence undercuts the maximalist read: he notes the artifact in his own video "was AI generated," and separately admits "even recently we have gone wrong" on source-of-truth quality. That is a system that produces volume and requires human judgment to stay correct — which is precisely Gerhardt's point.

### 4. Novelty vs. repetition: what should cadence optimise for?

- **Adam Robinson** optimises for range and freshness — the prolific zone, polarising takes, and vulnerability so far past the norm that his exec team told him not to post it *(source: [YouTube 16.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eUzqVJpZPP4) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/adam-robinson/eUzqVJpZPP4.md))*.
- **April Dunford** optimises for sameness — say it until you are sick of it, and resist changing messaging that is working *(source: [YouTube 02.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9pf_iEomjY) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/april-dunford/D9pf_iEomjY.md))*. Their observed behaviour matches their advice: Robinson and Braun post near-daily; Dunford posted once in the ~10-day window I sampled.

**My call: they are optimising different layers, and you need both — a fixed spine and a variable surface.** Your positioning claim should be near-verbatim identical across months (Dunford). The stories, hooks and formats carrying it should vary constantly (Robinson). The failure mode I would guard against is the one Dunford names: a team that gets bored of its own message and rewrites the spine, mistaking internal fatigue for market fatigue. Robinson's cadence without Dunford's spine produces a popular feed that never accumulates into a position.

## What I rejected and why

### 1. Rejected: Neil Patel's Gen Z research as an input to B2B SaaS planning

Patel's most-repeated finding in my sample — Gen Z needs 11 brand touchpoints versus 3 for boomers — is presented as a general marketing law *(source: Neil Patel, [LinkedIn 18.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7473345249931866114/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/7473345249931866114.md); [YouTube 17.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WQHJcSiTc7s) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/neil-patel/WQHJcSiTc7s.md))*.

**Why I rejected it:** read the supporting evidence and it is entirely consumer retail — TikTok Shop, pop-up shops, cart abandonment, Unilever's influencer spend, "travel and experiences" as a spending priority. The behaviour being measured is an individual buying for themselves with their own money. B2B SaaS purchases are committee decisions with budget cycles, procurement and security review; the "11 touches to earn a follow" metric has no defensible mapping onto them. The 11-touch number is also stated without linkable methodology. **I use Patel for distribution mechanics and dropped his audience research entirely.** Generalising consumer psychographics to B2B is the single most common category error in the collected set.

### 2. Rejected: Eric Siu's "company brain" as a starting architecture

Six layers, persistent memory at scale, 90+ daily crons, thousands of call transcripts *(source: [YouTube 19.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTuA_uMXfCQ) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/eric-siu/qTuA_uMXfCQ.md))*.

**Why I rejected it for this reader:** it is an answer to a problem of scale, presented to people who have a problem of traction. The architecture presupposes 2,800 sales-call transcripts — a pre-PMF founder has perhaps 15, and 15 calls do not need a retrieval layer, they need to be re-read. Three further reasons for skepticism: the video routes to Siu's own product (SingleBrain), the artifact he showcases as proof of the system was itself AI-generated, and he concedes the source-of-truth layer has recently failed for them. I kept exactly one layer — **capture** — because it is cheap, compounding, and useful at any size (§4.1).

### 3. Rejected: Adam Robinson's maximum-vulnerability play as a general tactic

Robinson's example is posting publicly about a layoff and about falling out of product-market fit with 18 months of unfixable churn, against his exec team's advice *(source: [YouTube 16.04.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eUzqVJpZPP4) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/adam-robinson/eUzqVJpZPP4.md))*.

**Why I rejected it as a recommendation:** the risk is asymmetric and he says so himself — "If you have investors, you probably can't go that far." He is a bootstrapped founder whose audience narrative rewards candour, publishing to 150k followers where the post lands as courage. The same post from an unknown seed-stage founder reaches prospects mid-evaluation and enterprise buyers running vendor risk assessment, where it reads as instability. I kept the *principle* (say the true thing others won't) and replaced the *mechanism* with something that carries the same signal at a fraction of the risk — see the Loss Ledger below.

### 1. Pitch-test the claim before you publish it **[ORIGINAL]**

Dunford tests *positioning* in live sales calls. Robinson sources *content* from creators, LLMs and Reddit. Nobody in the set proposes testing a content thesis the way Dunford tests a pitch.

**The mechanism:** before publishing any post that makes a substantive claim, say the claim out loud in your next three sales calls and watch what happens. Does the prospect lean in, argue, or go blank? Do they repeat it back in their own words later in the call — the strongest possible signal? Only publish claims that produced a reaction with live buyers.

**Why it should work:** it borrows Dunford's justification wholesale. Her case against page tests is that they confound variables and lack signal at low volume; a LinkedIn post has exactly the same defect, plus a worse one — its audience is mostly peers and competitors, not buyers. Three sales calls give you higher-quality signal than 3,000 impressions, cost nothing extra because the calls are already scheduled, and let you steal the prospect's own phrasing for the hook. It also inverts the usual failure mode: instead of publishing to find out what resonates, you publish only what has already resonated with someone who could actually buy.

**How it could fail:** live reaction measures *comprehension and interest*, not shareability. Claims that make buyers nod may be too consensus-safe to travel — a direct tension with Robinson's prolific zone. Mitigation: use the calls to validate that the claim is *true and legible*, and Robinson's method to decide how sharply to *frame* it.

### 2. The Disqualifier Asset **[ORIGINAL]**

Braun's trust mechanic is interpersonal: the mechanic earned trust by naming what the buyer did not need to buy. Nobody in the set turns that into a permanent, published asset.

**The mechanism:** publish one page and one short video titled, plainly, *"Who should not buy us."* Name the three segments you genuinely serve badly, name the competitor who serves each of them better, and be specific about why. Then structure it for machines as well as humans — plain HTML, explicit question-and-answer headings ("Is [product] a good fit for a 5-person team?"), and unhedged declarative answers.

**Why it should work:** three collected threads converge on it and none of the authors joins them. Braun supplies the trust mechanic — "here's what you don't have to buy" *(source: [YouTube 17.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9_BeKzJlOXs) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/josh-braun/9_BeKzJlOXs.md))*. Dunford supplies the raw material: a positioning exercise already forces you to identify obvious bad-fit customers, an artifact normally left in a workshop file. Patel supplies the distribution reason: buying-intent answers are increasingly assembled by AI systems before a human scrolls *(source: [LinkedIn 20.06.2026](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7474159287041249280/) · [archive](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/7474159287041249280.md))*. A disqualifier page is unusually well-suited to being cited by an answer engine because it makes falsifiable, specific, non-promotional claims — the kind of content that reads as reference rather than marketing. Secondary benefit: it pre-filters demos, which directly serves Braun's priority-rank problem (§1.6).

**How it could fail:** it hands competitors a quotable list of your weaknesses, and a founder who is wrong about their own bad-fit segments will disqualify winnable business. It also assumes answer engines reward this kind of content, which is my inference from Patel's directional claim, not a measured result.

### 3. The Loss Ledger **[ORIGINAL]**

My replacement for Robinson's layoff post — same candour signal, bounded risk.

**The mechanism:** once a quarter, publish a structured teardown of deals you lost. Not a confession: a pattern analysis. *"We lost 11 deals last quarter. Seven went to [competitor] because of X. Three had no budget. One we should never have pitched. Here's what we changed."*

**Why it should work:** it satisfies Robinson's prolific-zone criterion — almost nobody publishes losses, so it sits in the band where agreeing readers respect it and disagreeing ones argue — while avoiding the instability signal that sank my objection to his layoff example. It is also the only vulnerability format that is simultaneously *useful to the reader* rather than merely brave, because loss reasons are competitive intelligence for anyone evaluating the same category. Operationally it is free: the data is already in the Stage 0.5 question log and the Stage 3.2 positioning review, so the ledger is a byproduct of work you should be doing anyway. Feeds naturally into the Disqualifier Asset.

**How it could fail:** repeated publicly, a loss pattern that never improves reads as decline rather than candour. Cap it at four per year and only publish alongside the change you made.

## Weaknesses of this playbook

**The evidence base is thin and I would not defend it as representative.** One video per creator. Only 3 of 10 have a full 10-post LinkedIn sample; for six creators I have a single post, which means any claim I make about *their* cadence is unsupported by my own data. Everything was captured in one week of June 2026, so the topic mix is heavily skewed toward AI — that is what these people happened to be posting about, not necessarily what matters most.

**Every source has the same conflict of interest.** All ten sell something adjacent to the advice they give: courses, agencies, communities, software, coaching. Erhart's method requires a tool he is an affiliate for; Siu's architecture is his product; Patel's posts route to Ubersuggest; Robinson's video advertises RB2B twice. I have flagged this where it changes the reading, but I cannot correct for it. **There is no disinterested source in this playbook.**

**Severe survivorship bias.** I studied ten people who succeeded at building audiences. I have zero data on founders who ran identical playbooks and got nothing — which is the majority. The base rate is invisible to me, so I cannot tell you what any of this is worth in expectation.

**No outcome data anywhere.** Not one artifact links content to revenue. Engagement counts are the only measurable output I collected, and §3.3 argues those do not measure intent. **This playbook is therefore a set of well-reasoned hypotheses about mechanisms, not a validated system.** The strongest honest claim I can make is that these are the practices of people who are demonstrably good at this, which is not the same as evidence the practices caused the outcome — precisely Lemkin's point about his own 140%.

**The engagement figures I collected are partly noise.** Several posts were captured within hours of publication — one Braun post at 21 minutes old with 4 reactions. Those counts are not comparable to week-old posts and should not be read as performance signals.

**The 90-day sequencing is my assumption, not a finding.** No source proposes this order; I assembled it. The stage gates (5 pitches, 15 conversations, 3 customers) are reasoned thresholds, not measured ones. A founder with an existing audience should ignore the sequence entirely.

**My three original ideas are untested by me or anyone else.** I have argued why each should work and named how each could fail, but none has been run.

**Two structural gaps.** The honest-review/BOFU archetype named in the original research brief was never actually collected, which is exactly the evidence I would want for the Disqualifier Asset — so that idea rests on inference from Patel rather than on data. And Dan Martell contributed no LinkedIn data at all, because the handle in `creators.json` resolves to a different person.

**I have never run this.** I am not a B2B SaaS founder. This is a synthesis of other people's practice by someone who has not carried a quota.

## Who I would NOT recommend following, and why

### Primary: Dan Martell — not a B2B SaaS source, despite the positioning

He was ranked #2 in the original catalog on the strength of his SaaS Academy reputation. The collected artifact does not support that ranking.

**The evidence, from his own transcript** *(source: Dan Martell, [YouTube 18.06.2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dpO4mv9xHw0) · [archive](research/youtube-transcripts/dan-martell/dpO4mv9xHw0.md))*:

- **Word frequency in the full transcript: "invest" 48, "money" 52, "stock" 10 — and "SaaS" exactly 0.** The most recent upload from the channel catalogued as the benchmark for scaling SaaS founders does not contain the word once.
- The actual content is general wealth advice: index funds, owning equity, not trading crypto, spending money to save time. Useful or not, it is not B2B SaaS operating guidance, and much of it is indistinguishable from any personal-finance channel.
- Credentials are self-asserted and repeated twice in one video ("voted number one angel investor in Canada"), without a citable source.
- The teaching is wrapped in lead capture: the same "DM me the word YouTube EA on Instagram" appears twice, mid-video and again at the close.
- His LinkedIn handle in the catalog resolves to a different person entirely — an engineer with no posts — meaning his LinkedIn presence could not be verified at all.

**My recommendation:** do not use Martell as a source for this playbook's reader. His delegation framework (the buyback loop) is the one genuinely transferable idea, and it is explicitly staged *after* you have cash — which makes it irrelevant to a pre-PMF founder, and actively harmful if applied early, since founder-led content works precisely because the founder is the one doing it. **I would replace him in the catalog** with an operator who publishes about the mechanics of running a B2B SaaS company. I would not call the content dishonest; I would call it mismatched to the stated purpose, and I think the original scoring rewarded reputation over the artifact.

### Secondary, with qualification: Neil Patel — a distribution benchmark, not a strategy source

Patel earns his place on production discipline: 10 posts in 4 days, consistently structured, never off-cadence *(source: [Neil Patel, LinkedIn 17–20.06.2026](research/linkedin-posts/neil-patel/index.md))*. Study the machine.

Do not take the substance on trust. Three reasons: the audience research is consumer retail generalised to "marketing" (see Rejection #1); statistics arrive without linkable methodology — "we ran 4,300 prompts across 500 commercial keywords," "we looked at 60 campaigns" — which makes them unfalsifiable and conveniently supportive of the conclusion being sold; and nearly every post terminates in a CTA for Ubersuggest or NP Digital, including the AI-search posts whose implied remedy is his own tool. **Follow for cadence and format. Verify every number before you plan against it.**

April Dunford, Josh Braun and Dave Gerhardt are the three I would recommend without qualification. Each makes falsifiable, mechanism-level claims, each shows their reasoning rather than only their conclusions, and each is willing to say something against their own commercial interest — Dunford telling teams *not* to touch working messaging, Braun telling sellers to name what the buyer should not buy, Gerhardt platforming an argument that the AI tools his audience is buying produce uninspired work.
